The Platypus (Ornithorhynchus anatinus)

If you have ever watched Phineas and Ferb, you would be well acquainted with Perry the Platypus. Have you ever wondered what platypuses are? And, more importantly, what is it like to be these wonderful egg-laying, duck-billed, beaver-tailed mammals? Well, I took some time out of my day to learn more about them.

Platypuses are an endemic species, meaning they are limited to a specific region or part of the world. They are specifically found in eastern Australia, including Tasmania.

European explorers were the first to document the platypus in writing in 1797. These explorers sent back several preserved specimens home, prompting a series of anatomical studies. At the time, the origin of the platypus was highly debated, with many scientists believing it was a bunch of animals stitched together to create a fake animal. But alas, platypuses are very much real!

Interestingly, adult platypuses don't have teeth; they rely on heavily keratinised food-grinding pads, which allow them to eat a variety of insect larvae, yabbies, small fish, and worms.

Before August and September, females lay between two and three eggs in a burrow over 10m in length and incubate for at least 10 days before hatching. After hatching, the young are suckled for two to three months, after which they leave the nest in June/July. Fun fact, young platypuses are known as puggles!

Venom

Surprisingly, male platypuses have venomous spurs on their back legs and are one of the few mammals living to produce venom. The venom is not lethal to humans, but enough to produce excruciating pain and may be strong enough to incapacitate a human fully.

Electrolocation

The platypus is a unique mammal because it senses via electroreception—the ability to detect electrical signals from prey. While underwater, the platypus closes its eyes, ears, and nose and relies on about 40,000 electroreceptors in its bill to sense tiny electrical currents produced by prey movements. These receptors work alongside touch receptors, and the brain processes electrical signals similarly to touch, allowing the platypus to determine the direction and distance of prey. This adaptation is especially useful for hunting in murky water.

The platypus’s visual system is relatively weak and adapted for low-light aquatic environments. Its eyes close underwater, and its brain prioritizes electrical sensing over vision, reflecting an evolutionary shift from visual to electrosensory hunting.

Its hearing is well developed, particularly for high frequencies, and its ear structure is similar to that of other mammals. However, its sense of smell is less complex than that of echidnas, another monotreme.
