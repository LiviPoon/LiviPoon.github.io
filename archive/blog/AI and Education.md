How we educate, and what we define as a "smart" student, is changing. With the rise of AI, I believe schools would benefit from rewarding growth and perseverance over test scores, abolishing failing grades in younger grades, and shifting further toward experiential learning. It is time for schools to focus on EQ instead of IQ. Here are some ideas, let me know your thoughts!

1) *Teaching students to build a **growth mindset** is important now more than ever.*

Teaching students to build a growth mindset matters now more than ever. AI can complete assignments flawlessly, and anyone can learn new skills without paying tuition or setting foot in a classroom. In a world where polished answers are a prompt away, the value of education shifts from what students produce to how they think, struggle, and grow.

So what is the solution? Reward effort and iteration, not just final output. Grade the messy first draft, the revision, and the reasoning behind the changes. Let students wrestle with hard problems before reaching for a tool. Treat AI as a thinking partner that students learn to question, not a shortcut that does the thinking for them. Celebrate the productive struggle, because that is where real learning lives. 

The students who thrive from here on out will not be the ones with the cleanest AI-generated essays. They will be the ones who stay curious, embrace getting things wrong, and keep building skills the long way, because they understand that the process is the point.

2) *Student's would benefit from grades based on perseverance rather than scores on a test or assignment.* 

I believe that student's who improve their test scores, self-advocate, and demonstrate a growth mindset should be awarded more than the person constantly bombing or getting perfect scores. It is time to **abolish** the idea of the straight-A or failing student. 

We see it now more than ever that **good grades do not** guarantee jobs ([Deloitte, 2022](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/topics/talent/organizational-skill-based-hiring.html)), getting into prestigious colleges ([Forbes, 2026](https://www.forbes.com/sites/christopherrim/2026/05/07/thousands-of-students-with-perfect-40-gpas-get-rejected-from-the-ivy-league-every-year-heres-why/)), fair representation of student work ([Cain et. al, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10159463/#sec3)), or success in life ([Samuel, 2020](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/psychology-yesterday/202011/why-are-we-told-good-grades-lead-success-in-life)). 

We can shift our education system towards building the skills that bring forth long term success, foster healthy wellbeing, and develop strong leaders. Those skills are not measured by current grading systems.

3) *Young children need to fail and they need to fail in experiential contexts that encourages failures, problem-solving, and peer-to-peer interactions away from adult supervision.* 

Our generation's parents do not let their children out to play anymore and parents are more likely to shield their children from making mistakes rather then letting their child learn from said mistakes by experiencing them hands on ([NYT, 2020](https://www.nytimes.com/2020/01/08/parenting/teach-your-kids-to-fail.html)). I believe that young children do not benefit from sitting in a classroom and listening to teachers lecture about content they do not get to interact with hands on. Instead, we should create and foster environments where young children are free to make mistakes. As long as those mistakes aren't life-threatening, they should be made.

4) *For younger students, "failing" grades should be abolished.*  

For younger students, "failing" grades should be abolished. They do not teach a healthy definition of what it means to fail. What we should be teaching instead is to put in the effort regardless of whether you mess up, and to not let mistakes dictate how you feel about yourself. Strip the competition out of the equation. Focus on the growth of each student rather than the comparison between them.
